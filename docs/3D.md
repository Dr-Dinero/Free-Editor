# 3D that looks real

## Why code-built 3D looks cheap

A browser 3D scene built from boxes and tubes looks like a 2005 video game for reasons that have
nothing to do with effort:

- **No real light.** A browser scene is lit by a few point lights. Real renders are lit by a whole
  photographed environment, with light bouncing between surfaces. Without bounce light and
  reflections, metal looks like brown plastic and everything else looks flat.
- **No real surface.** One flat colour per object. Real materials have roughness that varies,
  fine bumps, edge wear and fingerprints.
- **Razor edges.** Real objects have slightly rounded edges that catch a highlight. Perfectly
  sharp edges are the fastest tell of a computer model.
- **No lens.** No depth of field, no motion blur, no haze. Everything is equally sharp, so nothing
  feels photographed.
- **Shapes made of primitives.** A snake made from a tube is a garden hose. Creatures, people,
  hands and cloth cannot be built from basic shapes.
- **Dark on dark.** With weak lighting the fix was to dim everything, which hides the object too.

## What to use instead

| Need | Tool | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Objects, logos, products, type, abstract shapes, rooms | **Blender (Cycles), run from a script** | Free, runs with no screen, physically correct light, real materials, depth of field and motion blur. A scene is a Python file, so it can be a reusable template: change the text or logo and render again. |
| Creatures, people, weather, fire, water, anything alive | **A video model** (image-to-video from a strong still), or licensed stock | No scripted 3D pipeline gets a believable animal. Generate or license it, then cut it in as b-roll. |
| Small 3D accents inside motion graphics (a card that tilts, a phone that turns) | **three.js done properly** | Only with a real model file, an environment map for lighting, physically based materials, tone mapping, and bloom and depth of field. Never primitives with point lights. |

## Blender rules

1. Light with an HDRI environment or large soft area lights: a warm key, a cool rim from behind,
   a low fill. Never a bare point light.
2. Bevel every hard edge.
3. Materials use the Principled shader with roughness driven by a texture, plus a fine bump.
4. Give the object a floor to reflect in, or it floats.
5. Use a long lens (50 to 85 mm) with a wide aperture so the background falls soft.
6. Render with motion blur for anything that moves; denoise; use the AgX or Filmic view.
7. Models come from a licensed library, not from primitives, whenever the subject is a recognisable
   thing. Record the licence beside the file.
8. Render the 3D as its own clip (with transparency when it sits over footage) and cut it in like
   b-roll. Never render it live in the page.
9. Render time is real: plan on tens of seconds per frame on a laptop CPU and a few seconds per
   frame on a recent GPU. Keep 3D moments short (2 to 3 s) and build them as templates that are
   rendered once and reused with new text.

## The freeze-frame test

Pause on the 3D frame next to the frame of real footage before it. If the 3D frame would not pass
as a photograph or as deliberate, polished graphic design, it does not ship. A measured pass from
the gate says nothing about this; a person has to look.
